How does the middle school work?

FASN welcomes students from 6th grade (sixième) to 8th grade (quatrième). Between 2.5 and 3 hours a day, depending on the school, are devoted to the French curriculum: every day, one American class period is replaced by a French curriculum class.

A typical day (middle school)

The day starts at 8:15 a.m. at Crossroads Elementary and Middle School and at 8:20 a.m. at Great Neck Middle School.

At Great Neck Middle School, students first attend their FASN classes before joining their American classmates, whose classes start at 9:30 a.m. Every day, one American class period is replaced by an FASN class.

The day ends at Crossroads Elementary and Middle School at 4:15 p.m. Monday to Thursday and at 2:45 p.m. on Friday, and at Great Neck Middle School at 4:00 p.m.

American curriculum

Students are integrated into American classes, alongside their American classmates. They have one teacher per subject. As each student has their own timetable, the group changes from one class to the next.

The subjects taught are those usually found in France: English, mathematics, science, history, geography, civics, physical education, the arts, etc. Teachers thus prepare students for high school by gradually developing the five competencies (5Cs):

  • critical thinking;
  • creative thinking;
  • communication;
  • collaboration;
  • citizenship.

Classes are mostly paper-based, and sometimes digital.

Important: the second modern language (LVB). In the United States, from 7th grade (cinquième), students can choose whether or not to take a second language. Since the LVB is compulsory in France up to the baccalauréat, French students are advised not to neglect their second language while in the United States. When a student arrives in the United States in 8th grade (quatrième), a short oral assessment may be required to determine the level they will join.

Language support. On arrival, and until their level of English is deemed sufficient, students are followed by a teacher who specializes in teaching English to non-English-speaking children (English as a Second Language program, ESL). This teacher helps them settle into the school and learn the language.

During their first year as ESL students, their American results are expressed as “Pass” or “Fail”. This way of grading eases their adjustment by limiting the pressure of grades.

French curriculum

Students are taught in the FASN French class. Classes are small. Teachers follow the curriculum of the French Ministry of National Education to ensure continuity in the acquisition of the knowledge, methods and skills specific to the French education system.

Advantages of this approach

  • avoiding any break with the French school system and making the return to French schools easier;
  • keeping the work habits acquired in French classes, as well as a strong command of French;
  • avoiding working alone at home, in favor of a group and a reassuring setting;
  • benefiting from extensive immersion in the American system;
  • settling into the American system more gradually than students enrolled only in that system, thanks to a group and French reference points;
  • lightening the load on parents and children, since the French classes are taught by FASN, with no correspondence courses;
  • developing students’ adaptability, as they gradually learn to move between two systems with different educational, social and linguistic codes;
  • taking part in educational projects that students could not join on their own, or in an American school alone;
  • building ties with American and French families.

Teachers of the French curriculum. FASN teachers build their lessons on teaching materials. They come from the French Ministry of National Education or from higher education and work at FASN either on secondment or as locally recruited staff, according to their field of expertise and level of education.

Status of the middle school. The Mission laïque française (MLF) guarantees that the teaching complies with French standards.
