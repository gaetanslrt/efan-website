What is FASN?

Founded in 2011, the French American School of Norfolk (FASN) meets the need for a French educational institution in the Norfolk area. The French military representative in Norfolk (Élément de soutien national, ESN) designed the project of creating a French American school. He sought the advice and expertise of John and Simone Ryan, a French American couple from Hampton Roads without whom the project could not have succeeded. Simone Ryan is, to this day, the founder and President of the school’s Board of Directors. The Mission laïque française (MLF), of which FASN is now a partner school, contributed its expertise to the initiative.

The Mayor of Norfolk, Paul D. Fraim, who was closely involved in the project, approved hosting the school within American public schools. The Norfolk Public Schools (NPS) Board of Education and the principals of the American schools concerned worked closely with the ESN and the MLF to build this French American educational partnership. At the start of the 2011 school year, FASN welcomed its first students at Crossroads Elementary School.

FASN is a hybrid school, an educational model unique in the United States. Its students follow the curriculum of the French Ministry of National Education, taught in partnership with Norfolk Public Schools (NPS) and Virginia Beach City Public Schools (VBCPS). They are enrolled in American public schools and share the daily life of American students, while receiving a French education.

All FASN teachers come from the French Ministry of National Education or from higher education. They work at FASN either on secondment or as locally recruited staff.

FASN embodies a dynamic collaboration between French and American educational traditions, in support of bilingualism, academic excellence and cross-cultural understanding. Through strong partnerships with Crossroads Elementary and Middle School, John B. Dey Elementary School, Great Neck Middle School and Frank W. Cox High School, the school offers its students a rich academic journey and formative social experiences that prepare them to thrive in a global community.

Why join FASN?

  • A French American curriculum. Your children keep following the French curriculum set by the Ministry of National Education’s Bulletin officiel in the subjects taught at FASN, and CNED materials for the others, while being immersed in American schooling.
  • Highly qualified teachers. All FASN teachers come from the French Ministry of National Education or from higher education, either seconded or recruited locally for their expertise and experience.
  • Demanding, recognized teaching. FASN, a hybrid school and an educational model unique in the United States, is a partner school of the Mission laïque française (MLF). Term report cards include the grades earned in both the French and the American curricula.
  • Individual support. Small classes allow teaching tailored to each student, whom the teaching team knows individually.
  • An easier return to France. Report cards, letters of recommendation and support, and a French working method spare students the re-entry tests of the French public school system.
  • A fast-growing community. Enrollment has grown more than sevenfold in six years, giving families many opportunities to meet.

Who is FASN for?

Students may be French or French-speaking. FASN also welcomes students who do not speak French, for whom starting between Kindergarten (grande section) and 3rd grade (CE2) is recommended. The school is not reserved for military families: it is open to all. In 2026-2027, out of 71 students:

  • 12 (17%) come from civilian families;
  • 5 (7%) are French speakers with at least one foreign parent.

The 71 students are distributed as follows: 25 in elementary school, 25 in middle school and 21 in high school.

How does it work?

Students, from Kindergarten to 12th grade, are enrolled in American public schools, in American classes. Depending on their grade, they follow 60 to 70% of the American timetable and share the daily life of an American student: school bus, lockers, lunch brought from home (lunch box), after-school activities.

These schools open their classrooms to the French teachers, who teach the French curriculum before, during and/or after the American school day. Depending on the grade, students miss only one American class period a day.

Common questions before enrolling

Is FASN a real advantage for returning to France?

Yes. FASN report cards, which can be certified, together with letters of recommendation and support, allow students to avoid the re-entry tests of the French public school system, which can delay the start of the school year and, in some cases, prevent admission to the school of their choice. FASN guarantees a French working method and supports families in choosing specialty subjects, registering for exams, preparing Parcoursup and Common App applications, and returning to France. It is also authorized to hold admission test sessions for schools that request it.

Is the dual curriculum suitable for every type of student?

Yes. Small classes allow personalized teaching and an excellent knowledge of each student. Students usually have less homework in the American curriculum, where homework is simply work not finished in class. FASN takes personalized plans into account and helps put 504 plans, IEPs and appropriate accommodations in place, together with the parents and the American teaching team.

Up to and including 10th grade (seconde), the combined timetables are comparable to those of a student in France, and teachers make sure to respect the students’ pace, in particular through the amount of homework given.

Will my child have the American experience at FASN?

Yes. Students are enrolled in American schools, integrated into American classes, and can take part in after-school activities within these schools: soccer, tennis, Greco-Roman wrestling, JROTC, band and the arts. They also take part in educational projects they could not join on their own, such as “Héritiers de mémoire”, the moral and civic education conferences, and mathematics, reading and public-speaking competitions. FASN also makes the transition into the American system more gradual than for students enrolled only in that system.

Will my child learn English like the other students?

Yes. Students are immersed in the American curriculum, in an American school and an American class. Like all international students, they take ESL (English as a Second Language) classes to perfect their command of the language.

Is FASN an accredited (homologué) school?

No. To be accredited by the French Ministry of National Education, a school abroad must have its own premises. When the school was founded, it was decided to place FASN within American schools, so that students could benefit from a genuine French American curriculum. Its operation is nevertheless very close to that of an accredited school: FASN report cards can be certified by the academic inspector of the French Embassy in Washington D.C., which attests to the quality of the teaching. FASN is a hybrid school, an educational model unique in the United States, and a partner school of the Mission laïque française (MLF).

For students in 9th grade (3e), 11th grade (première) and 12th grade (terminale), the way the brevet and the baccalauréat are taken differs from that of a student in France: in particular, students do not benefit from continuous assessment. The Parcoursup application is affected as well.

What skills does the dual curriculum develop?

The dual curriculum leads students to develop:

  • great intellectual flexibility: the substantial workload encourages independence and collaboration, across different teaching and assessment methods;
  • a stronger capacity for work: the dual curriculum requires rigorous time management and a high level of organization. It builds independence and the ability to handle a heavy workload, essential qualities for higher education;
  • a valuable international outlook: thanks to their dual educational and cultural immersion, students become fluent in English, which opens the doors of English-speaking universities, and gain the ability to adapt to multicultural environments and an analytical, critical approach shaped by French and American teaching methods;
  • transferable skills essential for higher education: independence and initiative, critical thinking and analysis, collaboration and teamwork, major assets for projects and academic research.
